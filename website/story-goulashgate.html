GoulashGate

Part II of III · A long story from the world of Dynamo Bresno · About 18 minutes

← Long Stories

Euphoria for the victory on the Saturday didn’t last long. By Sunday morning the Valrekian red tops and broadsheets were announcing scandal and investigation, depending on their readership’s leaning. By Sunday afternoon social media was rife with commentary and conspiracy theories. Come Monday morning, the scandal had acquired graphics packages on mainstream TV. That was how Tomas Montiselle knew the situation had become irreversible.

There was enough ambiguity in the first twenty-four hours post-match for the club to try and conduct damage control. The club statement had gone out quickly; title win a culmination of hard work, respect for the Velkovic Eagles and Union Dalnica for such a close-run title race, downplaying the significance of the outbreak, instead wanting to focus on football and a championship won through perseverance, dedication and grit. Standard containment language. The little good that it did.

Now the networks had themes. Animated paprika pots being shaken over food bowls rotated behind news studio desks. Tabloids printed digitally altered images of players in blue kits stirring giant cauldrons with question marks over them. One evening programme opened with mock espionage music while a presenter dramatically whispered: ‘Who poisoned the Eagles?’ Even state television had started calling it Goulash Gate without any sense of irony. The hashtag started trending soon after. The entire country appeared simultaneously outraged and delighted. Which, Tomas thought grimly, was usually how Valrekia consumed public scandal.

Outside Dynamo headquarters, reporters lined the street from dawn onwards. Satellite vans clogged the riverside road beside Le Centre Montiselle. Supporters gathered too. Some carried banners defending the club. ‘WINNERS ARE ALWAYS HATED.’ Others embraced the absurdity completely. ‘THIS TEAM ARE COOKING!’ Clara Jensen wanted security to remove that one immediately. Tomas told her to leave it.

“Why?”

“Because if we start confiscating joke banners, we look guilty.”

“We are guilty.” Tomas looked at her sharply. She lowered her voice. “Maybe not institutionally,” she corrected. “But enough people inside this building suspected something, and did nothing, to warrant the term being applied.”

That was the problem spreading through Dynamo now. Not merely suspicion and fear. Contamination and guilt. Every conversation from the previous month suddenly sounded different in hindsight. Win at all costs. Deliver the title. No excuses. The investment must be justified. Nobody had explicitly instructed Milan Gavinic to poison anyone. But the atmosphere around the club had begun rewarding extremity, and ambitious people often interpret pressure with creativity.

Inside the executive conference room, Milo Kolar smoked whilst watching the morning coverage on TV. It was a banned behaviour inside the club but Milo cared little for formalities and rules. Wisps of smoke drifted out of the open window he was stood beside, yet the smell still permeated the room. On a television a former Dynamo striker was arguing with the co-host about the benefits of Montiselle investment to Valrekian football. Kolar laughed and pointed his cigarette at the TV. “Another who will be expecting a little Christmas bonus,” he chuckled. “Who knew he had such beautiful turns of phrase.”

Clara remained unmoved. “You find this amusing?”

Milo shrugged and dismissed the question with a wave of his hand. “I find old farts like Dmitri going on TV and being the voice of the Valrekian media amusing.”

Clara continued to stare at Milo, unfazed. “Be that as it may, perhaps I can suggest you find our legal exposure more interesting and put some time and energy into addressing that.”

“Why?” Milo looked at her, genuinely questioning her statement. “The federation won’t do anything of importance. They are too worried that Montiselle would destroy them financially. So why put energy into something that won’t matter?”

Clara didn’t flinch as she bit back. “Because it may not be their choice what the outcome of this is.”

Everyone understood how deeply politics now infected the league. Montiselle investment had transformed Dynamo into both commercial opportunity and national irritation. The VFF might want an easy way out, but the broadcasters wanted spectacle, the league sponsors wanted stability, Union Dalnica and the Velkovic Eagles wanted punishment and Iron Bresno, well they licked their lips in anticipation of nothing less than total humiliation. Somewhere in the middle sat Tomas Montiselle trying to prevent the entire project collapsing into parody.

“Where is Milan?” Clara asked.

Milo exhaled smoke slowly. “At training. It’s the pre-summer assessments.”

She blinked. “Has he got a chaperone?” Kolar shrugged. For several seconds nobody spoke. Then Tomas laughed quietly. The amateurism of it all still surprised him at times. Of course, Milan Gavinic was doing what he had always done. He had spent thirty years surviving football through stubborn denial. He treated scandal the way old ship captains treated storms: if you kept steering long enough, eventually people called it resilience. And Milo Kolar hadn’t thought to do anything but dismiss it.

“He cannot talk publicly today,” Clara said.

“He’s the manager, people will ask him questions.”

Tomas interjected. “He’s potentially at the centre of a federal investigation.”

Milo shrugged again. “That’s tomorrow’s problem.”

“No,” Clara replied sharply. “Tomorrow’s a disaster, because today’s a problem. If he opens his mouth to journalists, who knows what trouble he will land us all in.”

Tomas stood slowly and looked over at Milo. He wasn’t happy with how the Director of Football was acting. He was behaving like a liability. In Tomas’ opinion it was time for the grown-up to step in. He nodded to the other two. “I’ll speak to him.”

The training pitch behind Le Centre Montiselle looked immaculate in the mid-morning sunshine. Tomas made a mental note to send commendations to the grounds team. Birds chirped softly in the trees along the edge of the training pitches, whilst thirty journalists screamed questions through the perimeter fencing. The Dynamo players trained with security watching over them. It reminded Tomas of coup attempts he had experienced in other countries.

Gavinic stood in a navy tracksuit beside the halfway line watching tactical drills as if nothing unusual was happening. That unsettled Tomas. The calmness of a man at the eye of the storm was never to be trusted. Gavinic acknowledged Tomas’s presence as he walked over. “You shouldn’t be out here,” Tomas said. Gavinic kept watching the session.

“Neither should half the journalists in this country, but life disappoints us all.”

“Milan.” Now he turned. He looked exhausted. Unshaven. Older somehow. But still defiant.

“You want me hiding?” Gavinic asked. “That would just look guilty.”

“You may already be guilty.”

Gavinic smiled faintly. “There’s the aristocratic honesty.” Tomas ignored the remark.

“You need to tell me what happened. We can’t do anything to control the situation if we don’t know what it is we are trying to contain.”

Gavinic stared back at him for several seconds. Long enough for the answer to become obvious even before he spoke. “I did what was necessary.” The words landed quietly. No drama, just a statement of fact. Tomas felt something cold move through him.

“You conspired to poison professional athletes.”

“I weakened opponents.”

“You committed sabotage.”

Gavinic finally showed irritation. “Spare me the moral outrage, Tomas. This entire league runs on corruption and pressure. This is nothing out of the ordinary.”

Despite himself, Tomas almost reacted. That was the danger of Milan Gavinic. Even now he could make catastrophe sound like nothing more than dressing-room humour. Gavinic gestured toward the players training nearby. “You think they care? They care about medals. Contracts. Europe. They care about winning.”

“That isn’t true.”

“It absolutely is.”

A nearby journalist shouted: “MILAN! ANY COMMENTS? HOW MANY SPOONS OF PAPRIKA DID IT TAKE TO WIN THE TITLE?”

Players laughed despite themselves. Gavinic smirked. “You see? The whole country thinks this is entertainment.”

“Because they don’t understand yet how serious this becomes.”

Gavinic’s expression hardened slightly. “And how serious does it become for you?”

There it was. Not denial. Not apology. Resentment. “You think I don’t understand this club?” he continued quietly. “You’ve built Dynamo into a luxury symbol. Fine. You want glamour. Prestige. Champions League football. All lovely.” He stepped closer. “But underneath all the fragrance diffusers and cinematic lighting, this is still Bresno. People here survive by doing ugly things first and apologising afterward.”

Tomas stared at him. For the first time since the scandal began, he understood the real divide between them. Gavinic believed he had protected the project. Tomas increasingly believed Gavinic had revealed it. Behind them, training stopped briefly as midfielder Liam Fitzroy doubled over laughing and the rest of the team paused to watch. Even now the squad treated the scandal with humour. It was becoming a legend forming in real time. Goulash Gate. The poisoned mountain men. The paprika conspiracy. All material for a joke on the training pitch or a supporter’s chant in the terraces. The sort of thing football folk would turn into folklore around Milan Gavinic, if given half the chance.

“Milan,” Tomas said carefully, “did anybody else know?” Gavinic looked away for a moment. And for the first time since the conversation began, hesitation appeared.

“That depends on what you mean by know.” Tomas felt his stomach tighten. “You applied pressure repeatedly,” Gavinic said. “All of you did.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“Oh, but it is Tomas. Pressure to get results, whatever it takes. I think you said that only a few weeks ago.”

Tomas turned and stared at his head coach. “You know full well that wasn’t permission to go act however you want.” Gavinic shrugged and then blew on the whistle he had been holding. The players froze and looked over at their manager. Tomas stepped back, ears ringing slightly. He took a moment to compose himself, looking around to make sure the watching journalists had not noticed his discomfort.

Gavinic started shouting instructions at the assembled players, who formed into two lines across the field. “Sprint drills. Go!” he barked, and the players obediently started shuttling up and down the rows. Tomas placed himself back next to Gavinic and touched him lightly on the arm. The other man half turned his head to indicate he was listening, whilst still watching his squad complete rotations.

“Let me be clear,” whispered Tomas, “no talking to the media. Do you understand?” Gavinic gave the slightest of nods. “Any comms or requests you receive, share them with Clara or myself immediately. We will deal with them. The situation needs containing, and we do that by saying nothing publicly and only what our lawyers tell us privately. We stick together and things will get better. We can get through this as a team.”

Tomas clapped Milan on the shoulder and made to move away. As he did so, Gavinic’s hand reached out and grabbed him by the wrist. Tomas looked at the eyes now intently starring at him. “That’s a team,” Milan said, indicating the players behind him. “You, me, Clara, Milo, we’re just a collection of individuals. I’ll do what you want, because you pay me, but mark my words, things aren’t going to get better, they are going to get worse. It doesn’t matter what you do.”

And with that Milan Gavinic let go of Tomas Montiselle’s arm and turned his attention back to the training session he was in control of.

By Wednesday the federation had formally launched its investigation. That changed the atmosphere overnight. The jokes stopped being funny. Seriousness reigned now. Security footage leaked to reporters showed shadowy figures accessing the National Elite Training Centre. Medical reports published by the Velkovic Eagles confirmed bacterial contamination. A young catering intern named Luka Bervic emerged as a central witness after reportedly “cooperating extensively” with investigators. The tabloids immediately descended upon him like starving animals. One paper described him as ‘the Kitchen Informant.’ Another: ‘the Boy Who Stirred the Pot.’ Valrekian journalism went into overdrive.

Inside Dynamo, paranoia deepened hourly. Staff whispered in corridors. Phones disappeared when senior executives entered rooms. Everyone suddenly remembered seeing suspicious things retrospectively. Even the players began fracturing. Some believed Gavinic had protected their season. Others felt contaminated by association. Captain Viktor Kosa reportedly told teammates: ‘If we had won honestly, nobody would need defending now.’ That quote leaked within four hours. Nothing stayed private anymore.

At 7:15pm Wednesday evening, Tomas received an encrypted message from an unlisted Montiselle channel. No sender. No signature. Just one sentence. ‘THE PROJECT IS THE PRIORITY INDIVIDUALS ARE NOT.’ He read it three times. Then deleted it immediately. But the meaning remained perfectly clear. Montiselle had reached its judgement. It was time for damage limitation, and that meant Milan Gavinic was now operationally expendable.

The emergency board meeting began Thursday morning beneath portrait of smiling Dynamo legends from bygone eras. They looked like they were thinking of happier, simpler times. Clara distributed printed briefing packs. Milo Kolar arrived late carrying an espresso and absolutely no remorse. Gavinic himself was absent. Intentionally. He was never going to be invited. The room felt like a tribunal held in secret.

“We have three simultaneous crises,” Clara began. “Legal. Commercial. Reputational.”

Milo raised a hand lazily. “No Footballing?”

She ignored him. “The VFF believes coercion occurred.” Tomas remained silent at the head of the table. So did the rest of the Dynamo management team. “The catering intern has apparently stated Gavinic pressured him directly,” Clara continued. “There are also allegations involving external consultants.”

Milo muttered, “Dragan, probably.”

Nobody acknowledged it aloud. Because once names started entering official conversation, liability existed. Clara turned a page. “Our sponsors are requesting reassurances.”

Milo laughed outright. “We ARE the sponsors,” he said, incredulous.

“Not all of them.”

Tomas finally spoke. “What does the federation want?”

Clare didn’t need to look at her notes to answer that question. “A public sacrifice.”

The room quietened immediately. Not justice. Not truth. Just symbolism. Valrekian politics operated theatrically and football was no different. Someone had to fall publicly for the system itself to survive with credibility.

“And if we refuse?” Milo asked.

Clara looked directly at him. “Then the investigation expands upward, step by step.”

Nobody spoke after that. The implication settled heavily across the room. Who knew? Who encouraged? Who created the atmosphere? Milo seemed surprisingly quiet.

After the meeting Tomas held back, looking toward the morning sun cutting across the stadium beyond the window. The refurbishment of the old Dynamo ground into the Stade du Cœur de Feu had cost north of a hundred-million, but it had been worth every penny. The ground now shone in the light, like the heart of fire it was named after. Three weeks earlier the project had felt unstoppable. Now the entire future of Dynamo Bresno might depend on whether one exhausted football manager could absorb national outrage alone. Deep down, Tomas hated himself slightly because he already knew the answer. Yes. He could, and he would. Because that was what football always did. Protect institutions. Consume individuals. Behind him Clara closed the briefing file softly.

“We need him gone.”

Silence followed. Not because anybody disagreed. Because saying it aloud made the betrayal real. Milo Kolar stared at the table for several moments before speaking.

“He’ll never forgive us.”

Tomas answered quietly.

“That stopped mattering the moment he poisoned the opposition.”

Outside, beyond the river, somebody had already started setting off fireworks again. In Bresno, scandal and celebration often sounded identical from a distance.

Gavinic found the stadium strangely peaceful in the early evening. The Stade du Cœur de Feu always looked simpler to him when it was empty. It was amazing how much humans could complicate things. Without supporters, without music, without the cinematic lighting sequences Montiselle insisted upon, the place reverted briefly into what it really was beneath the branding: concrete, steel, corridors, echoes. And a green pitch, upon which memories could be made and dreams lived.

He sat alone halfway up the west stand wearing a navy training jacket from before the takeover. The old crest version the Ultras still loved. The one without the refined gold detailing and luxury typography. Below him, sprinklers hissed softly across the pitch.

Beyond where he sat, the city of Bresno glimmered beyond the open bowl of the stadium. Eastward, across the river, the industrial districts remained lit in sodium orange, the colour of furnaces and heat. West Bresno, always the richer relation, felt colder now with its glass developments, riverfront apartments and Montiselle-backed modernisation creeping slowly across the skyline. The changing city was reflected by its football, or maybe its football was changing because of the city. It was hard to tell anymore.

Gavinic drank from a paper cup, in which the contents had grown cold. The scandal had entered its fifth day. Now came the dangerous phase. The shouting would continue. The media circus carry on. The outrage would still be there. But now the dangerous quiet element entered the game, for it was the turn of the administrators to cast judgement, and once they arrived, careers usually ended. Footsteps echoed somewhere high behind him. He did not turn immediately. He knew who it was. Only one person inside the club still walked that carefully.

Tomas Montiselle lowered himself into the adjacent seat with controlled elegance completely unsuited to football stadium plastic seating. For a while neither spoke. Hidden sprinklers began flicking water onto the pitch below.

“You know,” Gavinic said eventually, “I used to come here as a kid with my father, before the old west stand was demolished.”

Tomas nodded once.

“I know.”

“He hated Dynamo deep down.”

“That seems a…” Tomas paused for a moment, searching for the right word. ”…contradiction.”

Gavinic smiled faintly. “A contradiction. That’s one word for it, I suppose.” He looked out toward the pitch.

“He worked east river steel. Iron territory. He loved that world. But he still came here because he was born west side of the river. He didn’t have a choice and, even though Dynamo were the bigger club, played better football, had better players and more money, deep down he wished he could have been born on the eastside.”

“It’s a familiar story the world over.”

Gavinic laughed softly. “Exactly.”

“And what about you Milan?

Gavinic shrugged. “Dynamo are everything. I was born here, played here, mange here. For over forty years it’s all I’ve known.”

Neither man said anything for a while. Below them, water sprayed rhythmically across immaculate grass worth more annually than entire lower-league budgets.

“You’ve decided, then,” Gavinic said quietly.

Tomas did not insult him with denial. “Yes.”

Gavinic nodded once as if confirming weather. “Suspension first?”

“Yes.”

“Then termination.”

A pause. “Most likely.”

Gavinic rubbed his jaw slowly. “And you’ll all survive.”

There was no accusation in the sentence anymore. Only exhaustion. Tomas answered honestly. “The club will. Us is likely.” Gavinic looked at him finally. That irritated Tomas more than shouting would have. Because Gavinic understood. Truly understood. The project mattered more than the individual now. Dynamo Bresno had become too large to allow one manager to contaminate it permanently. Montiselle would amputate the limb to save the body. The logic was brutal. And from a governance perspective, entirely rational.

“You know the funny thing?” Gavinic murmured.

“I suspect you’re going to tell me.”

“I still think we would’ve won anyway.”

Tomas almost laughed despite himself. That was the madness of football management. Even now. Even after poisoning opponents. Even after national scandal. Part of Gavinic still wanted tactical credit.

“You cannot seriously believe that matters now.”

“It matters to me.” He leaned back in the seat. “You know what nobody understands about pressure at clubs like this?” Tomas remained silent. Gavinic continued anyway. “They think pressure comes from owners threatening you directly. But real pressure is quieter than that.” He gestured vaguely toward the luxury boxes surrounding them. “You walk through these corridors every day seeing the money spent. The expectations. The branding. The cameras. You know exactly what failure costs before anyone says it aloud.” Tomas listened carefully because the words struck uncomfortably close to truth. Gavinic smiled bitterly. “And eventually you stop asking what’s acceptable. You start asking what’s necessary.” Below them, the sprinklers shut off suddenly. The silence afterward felt enormous.

“You could have said no, you could have found another way,” Tomas said.

Gavinic nodded immediately. “Yes. I probably could have.” Nothing more. No justification. No excuse. Just a simple yes. And somehow that honesty made everything worse. From outside the stadium walls came distant chanting. Both men listened. At first Tomas could not distinguish the words. Then slowly they became clear.

MI-LOS. MI-LOS. MI-LOS.

Gavinic closed his eyes briefly. Supporters. A few hundred perhaps gathered beyond the main gates. Not protesting but defending. That was the final insanity of football culture: success distorted morality almost instantly. To sections of Bresno support, Gavinic had not poisoned opponents. He had fought for the city.

“Jesus Christ,” Tomas muttered quietly.

Gavinic gave a tired smile. “You wanted authenticity. Bresno’s giving it to you.” The chanting grew louder outside. Somewhere among the voices another chant emerged.

BRES-NO, BRES-NO, BRES-NO

The sound echoed strangely around the empty stand. Then stopped abruptly.

“Did anybody above me know?” he asked quietly. There it was at last. The real question. Not legal. Personal. Tomas considered lying. Instead he chose a careful truth.

“No one instructed this.”

Gavinic stared ahead. “That wasn’t the question.”

Tomas exhaled slowly. “The pressure around this club became unhealthy.”

Gavinic nodded faintly. “Convenient wording.”

“It’s accurate wording.”

“You all wanted monsters,” Gavinic said softly. “Just disciplined ones.”

Tomas felt anger rise for the first time all week. “No. We wanted professionalism.”

Gavinic turned sharply. “You rebuilt a workers’ football club into a geopolitical luxury project.” His voice remained calm, which somehow made it harsher. “You filled every wall with slogans about dominance and prestige. You spent fortunes telling everyone Dynamo Bresno was inevitable.” He pointed toward the pitch. “And then you hired football people raised in systems where survival always mattered more than ethics.”

Silence again. Cold, precise, dangerous silence. Because Tomas could not entirely reject the accusation. That was what unsettled him most about the scandal. Not merely the poisoning itself but also the recognition, the horrible possibility that Gavinic was not an aberration within the project, but one a logical outcome of it.

A phone vibrated in Tomas’s coat pocket. Clara. He already knew what the message would say before reading it. The VFF disciplinary panel had accelerated proceedings. Suspension announcement required immediately. Media leak imminent. The machine continued moving.

Tomas stood slowly. Gavinic remained seated. “When do we announce it?” the manager asked.

“Tomorrow morning.”

Gavinic nodded. Then, almost casually: “Milo knew more than he’ll admit.”

Tomas stopped. “What does that mean?”

Gavinic shrugged lightly. “It means football conversations become dangerous when nobody speaks directly.”

“That’s not evidence.”

“No,” Gavinic agreed. “That’s culture.”

Tomas studied him carefully. Was it true? Or a touch of paranoia? Or a desire for revenge, to take somebody else down with him in a twisted form of self-preservation? It was impossible to know anymore. That was the final damage scandals inflicted: certainty itself deteriorates.

“I won’t fight publicly,” Gavinic said.

That surprised Tomas. “You won’t?”

Gavinic smiled tiredly. “And become what? A martyr?”

Despite everything, Tomas laughed once. The sound vanished quickly into the cold stadium air.

“You know,” Gavinic said quietly, “when they built this place, I thought maybe Dynamo had finally escaped old Valrekia.” He looked around the vast empty arena. “But all we really did was give it a better façade. The old world still exists.”

Outside the gates the chanting continued. Some supporters now sang his name. Others would mock and scorn him in the days to come. Many would simply view the scandal itself as the latest episode of communal entertainment. GoulashGate. Already becoming part of the folklore, already mutating into myth. Tomas looked down toward the centre circle. In less than a week the club had transformed from champions into national embarrassment. And yet he understood something deeply uncomfortable now: the scandal would damage Gavinic permanently, possibly the club temporarily, but Montiselle itself? Montiselle would survive. Because power survived by doing what was necessary. Players and managers got photographed and interviewed whilst the owners stayed hidden. That was the real architecture of modern football.

Gavinic stood at last. For the first time all evening he looked aged, genuinely older than his years. “What happens next?” he asked.

Tomas answered honestly. “We sacrifice you.”

Gavinic absorbed the sentence without visible reaction. Then nodded once. “Yes,” he said quietly. “That sounds about right.”

The two men walked back toward the tunnel together through the silent stand. Above them, giant gold letters mounted beneath the roof glimmered faintly in the dark:

PRECISION. PROGRESS. PRESTIGE.

Gavinic looked up at them one final time. Then laughed softly to himself. Outside, somewhere beyond the river, fireworks exploded again over Bresno. In this city, nobody ever entirely agreed whether they were celebrating victory or merely enjoying the fall.
