The First Meeting

A short story from the world of Dynamo Bresno · About 5 minutes

← Short Stories

The training ground looked different in summer. The normally cloudy skies above Bresno carried no threat of snow or rain or cold, only heat and distant thunder rolling down from the Velkovic Mountains. Sprinklers swished, sending water droplets afar. Groundsmen moved silently between immaculate pitches. New banners bearing the Dynamo crest fluttered beside the navy-and-gold insignia of Atelier Athletiq.

Captain Viktor Kosa stood alone near Pitch One. He had arrived an hour early. He always did.

The younger players would arrive soon. Some nervous. Some arrogant. Some still believing football lasted forever. All wanting to make an impression and arrive as the boss did. Kosa knew better. Senior pros always did. The door behind him opened.

“You’re earlier than the staff.” The voice belonged to Anton Varga. The new manager carried no paraphernalia of a modern coach. No notebook, laptop or tablet. No assistants. No entourage. Just a black coffee and the expression of a man who had already been awake for three hours.

Kosa nodded. “So are you.”

Varga smiled. “Good answer.”

For a moment neither spoke. The training pitches stretched before them, empty and waiting. Varga gestured towards the small stand overlooking the grass. “Walk with me,” he said with a nod of the head. They climbed the concrete steps. Below them, sprinklers hissed across the turf. Varga leaned against the railing. “I first watched you play when you were sixteen. In the Lokomotiv academy.”

Kosa raised an eyebrow. “That long ago?”

“You were already impossible to get past.”

“People remember the tackles.”

“I remember the organisation.” Varga took a sip of coffee. “You spent ninety minutes pointing at other people, most of them older and tougher than you.”

Kosa laughed quietly. “My wife used to complain about that.”

“Smart woman.”

The humour faded. Both men understood why they were really here. The first difficult conversation of the new season. Varga stared out at the empty pitch. “I’ll be direct.”

“I’d be disappointed if you weren’t.”

The manager nodded. “My football is different.” Kosa said nothing. “I want intensity.” A pause. “I want pressing.” Another pause. “I want defenders who can sprint fifty metres in the eighty-fifth minute and still press the next ball.” The captain looked towards the far touchline. He already knew where this was going.

“Younger legs.”

Varga didn’t flinch. “Yes.” The honesty landed harder than diplomacy ever could. For several seconds only sound was the sprinklers and the faint thud of ground staff at work.

Eventually Kosa nodded. “How much?”

Varga turned to face him. “Less than before.” The words hung in the air. No manager ever tells a captain he’s becoming older. They simply stop picking him. Varga was choosing a different path. “I won’t lie to you.”

“I appreciate that.”

“You won’t start every match.” Kosa looked down at the grass below. The just one of the many pitches he’d defended over the years. The badge he’d worn through wins, defeats, injuries and triumphs. Most players feared this moment. The beginning of the end. But Kosa felt something unexpected. Relief. Because at least the truth had arrived at last and it was there in the open. No persistent rumours. No internal politics. No hidden agendas. Just football and the honesty of where he fitted in it all. Varga continued. “But.” The captain glanced back towards him. The manager’s eyes were fixed firmly on his. “I think you’re the most important player at this football club.”

Kosa frowned. “That sounds contradictory.”

“It isn’t.”

Varga placed his coffee on the railing. “When I look at the squad, I see talented players.” He pointed towards the academy building. “Hric. Jovanovic. Havel. Vanic.” Then the gym. “Kovalenko. Petrovic. Kovac. Simic.” Then the first-team complex. “Ferreira. Sebastian. Toscano. Al-Faraj. Karam.” One by one he continued to name the club’s emerging and established stars. “All good players.”

Kosa nodded. “Very good players.”

Then he turned back. “But when things go wrong…” He jabbed a finger gently into Kosa’s chest. “They look at you.” The captain remained silent. Because it was true. They always looked at him. After defeats. After red cards. After dressing-room arguments. After media scandals. Leadership wasn’t given by an armband. It was given by who everyone else turned to.

Varga walked over to the observation deck and sat on the front row of seats. “Let me tell you something.” Kosa joined him. “When I managed Red Star Polvan, I had an old midfielder.” The manager smiled faintly and ticked the fingers off on his hand. “Couldn’t run, couldn’t jump, could barely turn. Pace gone.”

“Sounds familiar.”

Varga laughed. “He played maybe fifteen matches.”

“Then why keep him?”

The manager’s expression changed. Because now he was speaking about something important. “Because every time he spoke, twenty-five men listened.” The answer sat heavily between them. Football clubs loved youth, they loved pace, power, passion, potential. But dressing rooms were built on something else. They were built on trust, and trust took years to build and forge. Varga looked directly at his captain. “I need you.”

“To play?”

“Sometimes.”

“To lead?”

“Always.”

The younger players began arriving in the distance. Cars rolling through the gates. Pre-season underway, a new era starting. Varga stood. “I’ll make difficult decisions this season.”

“I know.”

“Some people will hate them.”

“Definitely.”

The manager smiled. “And when that happens, I need somebody who understands what we’re building.”

Kosa rose beside him. The two men stood overlooking the training pitches. Manager and captain. One beginning a new chapter. The other approaching the final pages of his playing career. “One more thing,” Varga said.

“What?”

“When you eventually become a coach…”

Kosa smirked. “Eventually?”

“You’ll realise something.”

“Which is?”

Varga looked out across the empty field where players were now gathering. “The most valuable men in football aren’t always the ones on the team sheet.”

The captain followed his gaze. Young players laughing. Coaches preparing equipment. Senior pros stretching as they gossiped. A season full of possibilities. For the first time all morning, Kosa smiled. Not because he would play less. Not because he had accepted growing older. But because the new manager understood something many never did. Football matches were won on the pitch. Football clubs were built everywhere else. And, as the first whistle of pre-season echoed across the training ground, Viktor Kosa realised that Anton Varga hadn’t come to take something away from him. He had come to give him a different kind of responsibility.
