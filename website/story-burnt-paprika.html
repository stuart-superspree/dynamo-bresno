The Smell of Burnt Paprika

Part I of III · A long story from the world of Dynamo Bresno · About 20 minutes

← Long Stories

The problem with pressure, Tomas Montiselle often thought, was that it never arrived with great drama. People imagined pressure as shouting. Slammed fists. Threats in dark rooms. That was never the case, in his experience. For him real pressure arrived quietly and elegantly. It appeared suddenly, with little announcement of its arrival.

It sat expectantly at the end of polished tables beneath soft lighting. It spoke in calm voices and made impossible demands. It smiled whilst explaining that hundreds of millions of euros had already been spent. That European qualification was no longer aspirational but mandatory, and that finishing second would be interpreted, privately, as a weakness. And weakness, inside the Montiselle organisation, was not tolerated. It travelled like news upwards, fast and front page, only to return as pressure soon after. It was always the same, no matter how high you sat.

The Stade du Cœur de Feu glowed over west Bresno like a luxury liner moored alongside collapsing dockyards and old apartment blocks. Tomas believed it a beacon of modernism, of progress, standing tall against decades of under investment in Valrekia’s second city. A symbol of the benefits investment from the House of Montiselle would bring. Not all the city agreed. East of the river, smokestacks still stood above Iron territory as memorials to a world that no longer existed, and resentment festered within its industrial heartlands and warrens.

Inside the executive lounge, however, everything smelled faintly of cedarwood and expensive fabric. Milan Gavinic hated it. Not openly, never openly. But the hate was there if you knew where to find it. Tomas had begun noticing little signs over the previous months. The way Gavinic disposed of his jacket and tie immediately after sponsor dinners. The way he mocked the scented tunnel systems under his breath. The way he stared too long at Cattaneo’s giant cinematic player portraits hanging around the corridors, as if football itself had become an embarrassment.

Gavinic was old Valrekia football. Not old enough to be romantic about it, but then again, not modern enough to move forward cleanly. He was of an age to remember and wish; remember and wish for something that was never there.

“You understand what tomorrow means,” Clara Jensen said.

She sat opposite him at the long black table, posture perfect, tablet open, glasses low on her nose. Even exhausted, she looked composed enough to negotiate a state treaty. Gavinic lit another cigarette despite the rules. He cared little and revelled in others’ discomfort. Clara watched him but said nothing. Tomas stood by the window overlooking the pitch below as the late morning light shifted around the stadium.

“If we win,” Gavinic shrugged, “we win the league. What of it?”

“If you win,” Clara corrected calmly, “Montiselle validates three years of strategic investment.”

Gavinic gave a dry laugh.

“There it is.”

Tomas turned.

“Careful, Milan.”

“No,” Gavinic said. “No, let’s stop pretending. You rebuilt the stadium. You rebuilt the club. You rebuilt the media department. You rebuilt the bloody changing rooms, so they look like private airport lounges. Now you need a trophy to justify it all.”

Nobody answered. Because he was right. Dynamo Bresno had spent the last three years transforming itself from unstable sleeping giant into the most commented on project in Valrekian football. The aspiration didn’t stop there. Montiselle branding covered everything from the academy walls to the tunnel scent diffusers. International signings arrived with cinematic unveiling videos. Fashion magazines photographed players beside black helicopters. Money flowed and money talked. Yet still Union Dalnica remained level on points. Still the Velkovic Eagles remained dangerous, with an outside chance in third. Still the league table entering the final matchday looked unstable enough to make investors sweat.

The final matchday weekend was only a few days away. If Dynamo beat the Velkovic Eagles, at home, in front of their own fans in the Stade du Cœur de Feu, then the title was theirs. Anything else opened the door to failure and the potential for humiliation.

Outside, somewhere beyond the river, fireworks cracked over East Bresno. Iron supporters most likely. The city’s other team. Poorer, rougher, unsophisticated. Forever working class. Publicly hoping for a Dynamo collapse. Gavinic walked toward the window to stand beside Tomas.

“You know what they call this place now?” he asked quietly. Tomas didn’t need to answer, he already knew. Gavinic continued nonetheless, wanting to push his point home. “They call it the Velvet Dictatorship.”

“It’s creative, at least, I’ll give them that.”

Tomas studied him carefully. Milan Gavinic had the face of a former midfielder who had spent too much time outdoors and pacing too many technical areas. Weathered skin. A shaved skull to hide the early baldness. A permanent expression halfway between irritation and fatigue.

He had delivered results all season. But results inside Dynamo Bresno no longer existed alone. Everything now carried symbolic weight. Every dropped point became political. Every headline became geopolitical. Every trophy became proof of concept. Montiselle had not entered Valrekian football simply to participate. They had entered to dominate. And domination, Tomas increasingly realised, had a way of distorting ordinary people. Clara closed her tablet.

“There’s another issue.”

Gavinic exhaled smoke.

“There’s always another issue.”

“The Eagles are staying at the National Elite Training Centre in the run up to the game.”

Gavinic nodded once. Everyone knew the place. State-owned. Politically sensitive. Usually used by the national side. Facilities available at a price. Gavinic was impressed. The Eagles were not known for big budgets and spending largesse, so they must be taking the final game very seriously indeed, to spend the best part of a week in a training centre as costly as that.

“Security concern?” asked Tomas.

“Potentially.” Nodded Clara.

“Meaning?”

Clara hesitated very slightly. “The VFF requested increased precautions following supporter intelligence.”

Gavinic snorted. “The VFF thinks a banner is terrorism.”

“No,” Clara replied evenly. “They think Bresno supporters are unpredictable.”

“Same thing.”

Tomas looked between them. He felt suddenly tired. The club had carried this atmosphere for weeks now, the sense that nobody spoke directly anymore. Everything arrived wrapped up inside suggestion and implication. Pressure disguised itself as professionalism. Threats disguised themselves as targets. Fear disguised itself as ambition. Gavinic crushed his cigarette into a crystal ashtray.

“Relax,” he said. “Tomorrow, we win the league.”

“How?” Clara asked.

He smiled without humour. “By making sure we’re better than them in every way.”

That should have sounded reassuring. Instead, Tomas felt something cold settle quietly beneath his ribs.

Later that day, Milan Gavinic stood inside a service corridor beneath the National Elite Training centre, staring at six industrial catering containers. Steam curled softly from beneath the lids. Goulash. Traditional. Heavy. Spiced aggressively. The smell alone could knock a man sideways.

Beside him stood two men. One was Dragan Ilic, former club nutrition consultant, current unofficial fixer, and one of those men who always seemed to know which customs officers could be bribed and which journalists could not. The other was a nondescript catering assistant, who just also happened to be a staunch Dynamo supporter and Milan Gavinic fan.

The second man stood nervously, twiddling his white catering hat in his hands. Gavinic touched him on the upper arm tenderly, as he slipped an envelope into his hand.

“Luka, isn’t it?” The young man nodded. Gavinic smiled. “Well, thank you for your help, my friend. This is my little way of saying thank you.”

The second man grinned and nodded repeatedly, as if he had just won the lottery.

“Now Luka, can you give us five minutes and then return to collect the meal containers?”

The young man nodded. Milan smiled and then indicated with his head for him to go for a walk. Once the man was out of earshot, he turned to look at the six vats.

“You’re certain?” Dragan asked. It was Gavinic’s turn to nod.

“How sick?”

“Not hospital sick.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Gavinic looked at him. “Food poisoning,” he said flatly. “Not assassination.”

Dragan shrugged. “It depends on what effect you want to have.”

Gavinic almost smiled despite himself. That was the absurdity of it. The tiny ridiculousness at the centre of the entire enterprise. Not sophisticated espionage. Not military-grade sabotage. Just doctored goulash. The reigning tactical battle for the future of Valrekian football reduced to eating stew and the consequences that followed. Gavinic moved closer to the containers.

“Just enough to weaken them. What exactly did you use?”

“Bacterial concentrate. Fast onset. Nausea. Stomach distress. Dehydration.”

Gavinic rubbed his jaw. He hated details once things became real. It had sounded cleaner when the idea was first discussed. Earlier in the week it had sounded tactical. Velkovic played aggressively. High pressure. Relentless physicality. Conditioned to keep going, keep hassling and harassing for 90 minutes and beyond. They were exactly the sort of provincial club capable of ruining carefully managed season through sheer ugliness.

Union Dalnica would beat the Mariners comfortably tomorrow. Everyone knew it. If Dynamo slipped and didn’t beat the Eagles comprehensively, the title vanished. And Gavinic knew exactly what would happen afterward. Not immediately. Publicly, Tomas would defend him. Clara would call for stability. The media department would leak phrases like long-term project. Then, quietly, Montiselle would replace him with some younger European tactician, a bilingual strategist with a sharp jawline and data-led philosophy deck. The kind of man who looked better in cinematic tunnel shots. Gavinic had seen enough football to recognise replacement before it arrived. Dragan checked his watch.

“The service team leaves in twenty minutes. We need young Luka to get these up to the kitchens.”

Gavinic stared at the containers. His father had worked thirty-three years inside the Zreska rail foundries before drinking himself to death after privatisation. His first coaching job had paid him in cash and cigarettes. He had survived dressing rooms where players fought each other at half-time over unpaid bonuses. And now here he was. One match from the league title. One match from proving he belonged in this strange new luxury version of football.

He placed a hand on the steel container lid. Warm. Heavy. A moment of choice.

“You know what the worst part is?” he murmured. Dragan raised an eyebrow. “If this works, they’ll think it proves they were right about everything.”

The first Velkovic player vomited at 10pm. By 2am, seven were ill. By 5am, the medical staff were panicking. By breakfast time, the canteen was near empty and National Elite Training Centre smelled faintly of disinfectant, sweat and paprika. Assistant coaches ran through corridors carrying electrolyte packs. Security officers argued with kitchen staff. Someone from the federation kept shouting into a phone about contamination protocols. One reserve goalkeeper reportedly fainted while trying to reach a toilet. The local breakfast television presenter later described the scenes as: ‘a cross between military quarantine and the world’s least dignified spa retreat.’

The phrase spread nationally within hours. #GoulashGate was born before kick-off.

At 8:55am, Tomas Montiselle received a call. He listened silently. He ended the call, then closed his eyes. Across the meeting table, Clara Jensen stopped buttering toast.

“What happened?”

For several seconds he said nothing. Then he opened his eyes. “Velkovic’s squad has food poisoning.”

Clara frowned immediately. “How many?”

“Enough.”

She understood instantly. That was what frightened him most about her sometimes. Not cruelty. Nor coldness, or harshness or laser focus. Rather clear, calm competence.

“How bad?” she asked.

“Apparently catastrophic.”

“Hospitalisations?”

“Not yet.”

Clara stood immediately.

“Tomas…”

“Yes.”

“Do you think…”

“Yes.”

The silence that followed felt enormous. Outside Bresno shimmered beneath pale morning rain. Across the river, East Bresno remained grey and industrial and hostile. Somewhere beyond the skyline, Iron supporters were probably already hearing the news from relatives and friends who worked in the national centre.

Tomas suddenly imagined how this would look from outside: The luxury club. The foreign ownership. The expensive signings. The final-day title race. The mysteriously poisoned opposition. It sounded less like football and more like bad political satire. Clara was already moving.

“We need to contain this immediately.”

“You think we had something to do with this?”

“I think there are certain people who are desperate enough to think this was a good idea.”

Tomas stared at her.

“And if this came from above?”

Clara paused. There it was. The real fear. Not who had done it the deed. But whether someone inside Montiselle believed they should. Neither spoke for several seconds. Both understood the project they worked for. They understood the expectation to win, beautifully, visibly and inevitably, and in turn they felt the constant demand for domination. Failure inside Montiselle rarely arrived as punishment. It arrived as removal. And sometimes people did terrible things long before anybody needed to ask them to.

The match should never have gone ahead. That became the national consensus five days later. At the time, however, nobody inside the Dalnic Premier League wanted to be the person responsible for postponing the biggest title decider Valrekia had hosted in over a decade.

Broadcasters had arrived from across Central Europe. Montiselle had transformed the Stade du Cœur de Feu into a cinematic showcase for the future of the league. Drone rigs circled above the stadium. Giant navy-and-crimson banners hung from apartment rooftops in West Bresno. Even the rain seemed curated.Inside the Velkovic dressing room, meanwhile, two players were still taking turns vomiting into medical bins. The contradiction captured modern Valrekian football perfectly. Luxury above. Chaos underneath. By midday the VFF emergency delegation had arrived at the stadium.

Officials in dark coats hurried through service tunnels trying to project authority while clearly improvising everything. TV punditry and social came alive with rumours, conjecture and conspiracy theories; salmonella, sabotage, water contamination, betting syndicates, Dynamo ultras disguised as catering staff, political interference, Montiselle insurance policies. One fan outside the stadium leaned into the television camera and told the reporter: ‘they’ve poisoned the mountain men because they can’t outplay them.’ Another laughed and termed it: ‘an unfortunate incident and a Dynamo tactical godsend.’ The clips reached three hundred thousand views before kick-off. Consensus built that the Velkovic Eagles were going to be hard pushed to put a competitive team out.

Inside his office beneath the west stand, Tomas watched the coverage in silence. Clara stood beside the television with arms folded. Neither had slept well and tiredness was adding an edge to irritable tempers.

“The VFF wants to delay kick-off by fifteen minutes, but I told them to go to hell. They’ve had days to make a decision. I have already made the offer to postpone the game and give the Eagles time to properly prepare, but it was rejected. The VFF said they would consider options but aren’t interested in revenue loss and media criticism.”

Tomas nodded at Clara’s update. “I wouldn’t have expected them to respond in any other way. Money is what matters most to them. So, is there any chance they will they postpone last minute?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because the federation president has already arrived for the trophy presentation. That why they wanted the 15 minutes, to give him more time to get in position for the cameras.”

Tomas laughed once through his nose. There it was again. Valrekia. A country where institutional theatre was performed with absolute seriousness, right up until the point the money became threatened.

“Milan denies involvement,” she said.

“Of course he does.”

“He says it’s hysteria.”

Tomas gave a tired smile. “It stopped being hysteria once the ambulances arrived and the cameras were there to witness it.”

Down below, through the reinforced glass, Dynamo supporters filled the lower bowl in deep navy and crimson. Smoke drifted upward from flare clusters. Chants rolled around the stadium in slow waves. Gavinic’s name echoed from the south stand. Not affectionately. Triumphantly. That worried Tomas more than anger would have. Football supporters could tolerate almost anything if it brought trophies. Clara muted the television and looked towards the pitch.

“If Dynamo win this now…”

“I know.”

“No,” she said quietly. “I don’t think you do.”

That made him turn. She rarely spoke emotionally. “If this becomes deliberate sabotage,” she continued, “then the entire Montiselle project changes overnight. We stop being ambitious outsiders. We become the foreign ownership who poisoned a football team. To win.”

The last two words hung in the air. Winning was everything, but how you won, and were perceived to have won, were the critical elements. Tomas turned and stared at the television screen. A split-screen debate had already begun between former players and political commentators. One side called the situation unfortunate. The other termed it ‘the consequence of football colonialism.’

Even now, Tomas almost admired the phrasing. “Do you think Milan acted alone?” he asked. Clara took slightly too long to answer.

“Yes.”

He studied her carefully.

“You don’t sound certain.”

“I’m certain enough.”

Which meant she wasn’t. That was the real poison spreading through the club now. Not bacteria. Suspicion. Who knew? Who suspected? Who chose not to ask questions? Inside an organisation built increasingly around controlled image and silent influence, certainty had become difficult to locate.

Milan Gavinic walked into the dressing room forty minutes before kick-off to the sound of forced confidence. Music played loudly to boost energy. Several players laughed too hard. Nobody mentioned Velkovic directly.

Captain Viktor Kosa sat taping his wrists slowly.

“You hear how many are sick?” Jacek Kowalski whispered to him. Kosa shrugged, like he had seen it all before.

“Half the squad apparently.”

Kowalski shook his head and smiled. “Convenient.”

Gavinic slammed a tactics folder onto the central table. “Enough.”

Silence. He looked around the room. This was his team. His season. His league title. He had fought to bring this group together, worked tirelessly to forge bonds and install a never say die attitude. He had created winners, and now it was his time to step into the spotlight and bask in the glory. ‘Title winning manager’. It had a nice sound to it.

Just one last match to win.

And yet, the closer he came to achieving it, the less ownership he actually felt. Dynamo Bresno no longer belonged fully to football people. It belonged to executives and narratives and investment decks and cinematic launch videos. Winning today would not really belong to the players either. It would belong to Montiselle. Gavinic suddenly hated them all a little. “Listen carefully,” he said. The room focused. “Out there, nobody gives us anything. No respect. No easy rides. No forgiveness. No title. You think Union Dalnica wanted us here? You think the federation wanted Bresno lifting that trophy instead of the capital?”

Several players agreed by shaking their heads. Footballers always preferred a touch of conspiracy. Gavinic paced slowly. “You know what they say about this club. Foreign money. Luxury football. Plastic prestige.” Now more heads nodded. “But today we take the title anyway.” He stopped beside Kosa. “And when they talk about this season years from now, nobody remembers the noise. They remember who won.”

That line landed exactly as intended. Players straightened. Adrenaline returned. Purpose replaced anxiety. Belief washed over the room. Only Kosa continued watching him carefully. The captain had spent enough years in dressing rooms to recognise when a manager was performing certainty rather than feeling it. As the players rose to leave, Kosa caught Gavinic quietly.

“Boss.”

“What?”

“You look terrible.”

Gavinic smirked faintly. “That’s management. You should try it some day.”

“No, thanks” Kosa replied. “I’m not sure I want to be the person it turns you into.”

For the first time all day, Gavinic genuinely lost composure. Only briefly. Only enough for Kosa to see it. Then the mask returned. He placed his hand on the centre backs shoulder in an act of forced light heartedness.

“Well that’s your choice, your future. Today you’ve got one job; win the game,”

Velkovic looked like survivors of a minor maritime disaster. Their manager emerged from the tunnel grey-faced with fury. Two substitutes reportedly had fevers. One midfielder played after receiving intravenous fluids less than two hours earlier. And still they nearly ruined Dynamo’s day. That became the cruellest irony of the entire scandal. Even half-poisoned, the Velkovic Eagles remained horribly difficult to play against.

The mountain club defended with exhausted determination and touch of intentional brutality. Players cramped repeatedly. Their goalkeeper vomited behind the advertising boards after twenty-six minutes yet still continued. The whole team were determined to make a difference. Every time Dynamo playmaker Stefano Toscano got on the ball, he was battered and fouled to disrupt play and momentum. Every time they managed to get it out wide to Karim or Martenne out on the wings, the Dynamo players were harassed by two or three Eagles players into cul-de-sacs and dead ends.

At half-time the score remained 0-0. Inside the executive lounge, Clara stared at the pitch in disbelief. “This is becoming worse.” Tomas rubbed his forehead. Because she was right. If Dynamo had swept aside weakened opposition comfortably, the controversy might eventually have blurred into sporting cynicism. The skill, grace and elan of Dynamo’s team should have been too much for a fully fit and functioning Eagles team. Instead, the match looked grotesque. Sick men slogging it out, dragging themselves through ninety minutes while an entire nation watched. And yet, somehow, defying their opponents. It had an underdog story written all over it, and that made Dynamo the bad guys.

At one point television cameras accidentally captured a Velkovic substitute sprinting directly from warm-up drills toward the tunnel clutching his stomach. The image became iconic within hours. Online, hashtags exploded: #GoulashGate #TheyShallNotPass #PaprikaPlotThickens

The second half started as the first had ended. The stalwart Eagles backs against the wall, defending valiantly, defying endlessly. The reality though was that they couldn’t sustain their energy levels and hold out forever. Even a flurry of substitutions in the sixty-third minute failed to hold back the inevitable. In the seventy-second minute Dynamo finally scored. It was a terrible goal, an ugly goal, the sort of match-winning goal nobody remembers fondly. A deflected cross. Goalmouth panic. The goalkeeper making an instinctive reflex stop. A defender lunging desperately to block the follow up. Then the ball ricocheting off Aleksandr Kovalenko’s knee into the unguarded net.

The stadium erupted. Noise crashed upward beneath the floodlights. Pyro erupted behind the south stand. Gavinic punched the air so hard he nearly lost balance. For one delirious second, relief consumed everything else. Then he looked across toward the Velkovic bench. Their assistant physio was bent over vomiting beside the dugout. And suddenly the goal felt filthy.

The final twenty minutes crawled. Every misplaced pass increased paranoia. Every whistle intensified hostility. Dynamo added a second when Raul Ferreira headed in from a corner. Velkovic players screamed at referees. Dynamo players pushed back. One exhausted Eagles defender tugged Farid Karim’s shirt as he broke clean through, and the ref had no choice but the brandish the red card.

Down to ten men the Eagles went into damage limitation mode, getting everyone behind the ball. Yet, having put so much into the early part of the game, their energy lessened and their concentration weakened. Before they knew it Liam Fitzroy had belted in a screamer from outside the area to make it three-nil, and then, in the dying moments, substitute Kenji Sato, who had struggled to settle in Valrekia since moving from Japan in preseason, popped up to score an unlikely fourth and seal both victory and the league for Dynamo.

Eagles players thumped the turf in frustration. Their captain squared up to Viktor Kosa and a shoving match ensued. The referee booked both men. Tempers were frayed and closing to being lost. The referee played three more minutes and then blew his whistle. At the sound of full-time, the stadium erupted again.

After years of waiting, Dynamo Bresno were champions of Valrekia.

The players collapsed together near the centre circle while gold fireworks burst above the river. Music thundered through hidden stadium speakers. Giant LED displays flashed: PRECISION. PROGRESS. PRESTIGE. DYNAMO BRESNO. 2025/26 LEAGUE CHAMPIONS.

Yet for all the pomp and razmataz, the celebration never fully connected emotionally for some. Too many people were watching each other instead of living the moment. Velkovic staff refused handshakes. The VFF officials disappeared immediately down the tunnel. Journalists sprinted toward medical personnel instead of the trophy platform. Near the halfway line, Gavinic stood alone briefly amid the chaos. League champions. He had done it. And somehow it already felt like the beginning of something ending.

The trophy presentation began nineteen minutes late because federation lawyers were arguing beside the hospitality elevators. Nobody officially confirmed that. But everyone seemed to know. By the time captain Viktor Kosa lifted the silver championship plate into the Bresno night, the atmosphere had shifted from celebration into theatre. Supporters still roared. Players still bounced together singing. Confetti still fell. Camera bulbs flashed. But now thousands of phones pointed not at the trophy, but rather at the Velkovic bench. At the exhaustion. At the scurrying medical personnel. At the white lips and even greyer faces.

Television commentators spoke in cautious euphemisms: ‘difficult circumstances,’ ‘a strange final day,’ ‘unusual developments,’ ‘the most disputed championship trophy in years?’ ‘Questions likely to follow.’

Questions. Always questions and comments in football. Answers sometimes arrived later, thought more often it was conjecture or opinions, but it resulted in somebody useful being sacrificed to a baying crowd.

Gavinic climbed the podium last. As manager, protocol required him beside Tomas Montiselle for the official photographs. Smiled mechanically. Accepted the medal around his neck. Then he turned toward Tomas and saw it immediately. Not anger, nor panic but distance. Tomas shook his hand for the cameras with perfect public warmth. But his eyes had already moved elsewhere. Gavinic understood football politics instantly. He always had. That look meant: you have become a problem.

Below the podium, Clara Jensen spoke urgently into her phone while federation delegates crossed the pitch toward the tunnel surrounded by security. The investigation had already started, amidst the celebrations. Gavinic looked out across the stadium one final time. Smoke drifted through the floodlights. Supporters screamed his name. Somewhere beyond the river, East Bresno would be seething, frustrated at their rivals success.

Yet underneath the roaring celebration, almost hidden beneath music and fireworks and triumph, Milan Gavinic realised something quietly devastating: he had poisoned a football team to prove he deserved this level. And the irony was that the people above him had already decided he probably didn’t. The gold confetti kept falling anyway. The supporters kept chanting his name. But Gavinic knew in his heart of heart that this was the end of more than just the season.
