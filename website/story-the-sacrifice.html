The Sacrifice

Part III of III · A long story from the world of Dynamo Bresno · About 10 minutes

← Long Stories

The suspension statement took nineteen minutes to write and three days to negotiate. That, Clara Jensen thought, explained modern football perfectly. Officials on all sides cared more about the wording and positioning that then poisoning, scandal or institutional panic. Every sentence passed through lawyers, federation consultants, Montiselle communications staff, and two separate crisis-management specialists from Geneva, before appearing publicly on the Dynamo Bresno website at 09:03 on a rain-heavy Monday morning. It was followed by formal statements on the Valrekian Football Federation and Dalnic Premier League sites soon after.

The final version achieved exactly what these statements always aimed for: maximum distance disguised as professionalism and judgement. There were statements regarding ‘concerns regarding conduct’ and ‘pending investigation’ with ‘positive cooperation with the authorities.’ Nobody ever simply wrote: ‘our manager poisoned the opposition before a title decider and now the entire country thinks we’re football fascists.’ Though privately, Clara suspected that version would have tested better with supporters.

She stood inside the media office overlooking that overlooked the busy main road in front of the stadium. She made a note to demand better offices for the team once this scandal was over. On the other side of the room Cassandra Vellor had a team monitoring social reaction across six screens simultaneously. “How bad?” Clara asked.

Cassandra did not look up. “Depends which demographic.”

“Meaning?”

“Traditional supporters think the federation is targeting Bresno because of Montiselle.”

“And everyone else?”

“They think we’re evil.”

Clara nodded slowly. Reasonable summary. Outside the Dynamo stadium, protesters and supporters had merged into one chaotic crowd, countering each other’s chants and demands. Some carried banners demanding lifetime bans, immediate relegation or a big enough fine to initiate financial armageddon . Others wore shirts reading: FINE MARGINS WIN TITLES. One old man had arrived wearing a sandwich board that read JESUS SAVES on the front and DYNAMO ARE THE DEVIL on the back. Television crews followed him constantly.

The entire scandal had developed an atmosphere halfway between constitutional crisis and travelling carnival. That was Valrekia. Public outrage always risked becoming entertainment if exposed to enough alcohol and cameras. Cassandra finally looked up from the screens.

“The federation hearing is Wednesday.”

Clara exhaled slowly. Too soon and too fast, which meant political pressure had escalated behind the scenes. Somewhere inside Dalnica, powerful men had decided the scandal needed visible resolution before it contaminated sponsorship negotiations, league television deals, or UEFA relations further. Football governance rarely moved quickly unless money became frightened.

“Has Milan responded?”

“Refused media requests. Left his apartment once. Bought cigarettes and bread. Photographed both times.”

“Players?”

“Divided.”

That worried Clara most. Because dressing rooms understood morality differently from institutions. Half the squad considered Gavinic reckless and humiliating. The other half considered him a hero for winning the title, no matter what the methods. Neither interpretation helped rebuild the club.

The VFF headquarters in Dalnica smelled faintly of old carpet and a musty dampness. It was housed in one of the old socialist buildings in the centre of Dalnica, the capital of Valrekia. When Tomas Montiselle arrived on Wednesday morning it was already beginning to drizzle. Reporters crowded the entrance the moment his chauffeur opened the limousine doors: “Chairman, did the board know what Gavinic was up to?”

“Was Montiselle involved?”

“Should Dynamo be expelled from Europe?”

“What’s your view on serving Goulash at home games in future?”

That last question almost made him smile. Inside the federation building, portraits of former league presidents lined dark wooden corridors like stern ancestors. Clara walked beside him carrying three folders and no visible anxiety.

“How exposed are we?” Tomas asked quietly.

She answered without hesitation. “Operationally? Contained.”

“And morally?”

Clara glanced at him briefly.

“We stopped discussing morality five meetings ago.”

A fair point. They entered Hearing Room B at precisely 10am. Gavinic already sat alone at the far end of the long table. For the first time since the scandal began, he looked defeated, exhausted beyond the point of resistance. He wore a dark suit that was slightly too old-fashioned and out of kilter with modern football executives. No club badge. No Dynamo colours. Deliberate. A man already halfway erased institutionally. Tomas made a note to pass Milan the number of his tailor.

Several VFF officials shuffled papers theatrically while legal observers filled the rear rows. Cameras were banned officially, and the session was marked ‘confidential’, though everyone knew half the room will have leaked information before lunch. At the centre sat federation president Aleksandra Ivanova, iron-haired, precise, and carrying the expression of a woman who had spent decades disappointed by her footballing brethren.

“Let us proceed,” she said.

No introductions, no ceremony, just straight to business. The investigation summary lasted forty-three minutes, covering areas such as security breaches, witness testimony, bacterial contamination, public phone records and payments, plenty of payments. The details sounded pathetic when stripped of their football folklore. No grand corruption. No sophisticated espionage. Just worried people making stupid decisions beneath impossible pressure.

At one point an investigator stated they were 97.2% certain that the ‘severe contamination was likely to have been human in origin.’ Gavinic laughed quietly under his breath. Every head turned toward him.

“Something amusing, Mr Gavinic?” Ivanova asked coldly.

He rubbed his eyes tiredly. “No. Just remarkable how accurate someone can be in their opinion.”

Even now, even here, still the dressing-room humour. Tomas felt more saddened than angry, because this was the final stage of a football downfall, when a man continued performing their old persona long after it had stopped protecting them.

The hearing paused briefly before lunch. As officials exited, Gavinic remained seated staring at the polished table surface. Tomas approached slowly. “You should have brought a lawyer,” he said quietly.

Gavinic smirked faintly. “Why? The verdict arrived last week.”

Tomas sat next to him. For several seconds neither spoke. Outside the hearing room windows, Dalnica disappeared beneath grey and government architecture. Finally Gavinic leaned back. “You know what annoys me most?” Tomas expected anger, or bitterness, or maybe even self-pity. Instead Milan said, “we would’ve beaten them.”

Tomas closed his eyes briefly. Of course. Still that. Still the football man beneath the scandal, holding on to the belief they were all conquering, even in the face of evidence to the contrary.

“You really believe that?”

“Yes.” Gavinic looked toward the rain-streaked window. “And now nobody will ever know.”

That, Tomas realised suddenly, was the private tragedy consuming Gavinic. Not the disgrace, that could be overcome, but rather the historical contamination, the fact the history books would never recognise the superiority of Dynamo’s ability. Every tactical achievement from the season would now carry invisible questions marks and an asterix stating terms and conditions apply. League title?* possible consequence of cheating. Best attack in the division?* subject to fully fit opponents. Brilliant run-in form?* depends where the opposition stayed before hand.

“You could apologise,” Tomas said quietly.

Gavinic shook his head immediately. “No one would believe it.”

“That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t.”

Gavinic studied him carefully. “You sound like a politician, Tomas.”

“Occupational hazard.”

“No,” Gavinic replied softly. “Montiselle hazard.”

The afternoon session destroyed what remained of Dynamo’s position publicly. The catering intern testified remotely behind a privacy screen, to protect what little identity he had left. His voice shook throughout. Afterwards reporters would describe him as young, terrified, overwhelmed, and naïve, very naive.

He described Gavinic pressuring him repeatedly. Telling him the city depended on the result. Telling him ‘everyone important’ expected Dynamo to deliver the title and he would be thanked by the highest for his contribution. That phrase landed heavily across the room. Everyone important. Clara wrote something down immediately. Legal concerns to issue. Tomas shifted in his seat. Reputational distancing needed.

Gavinic himself barely reacted. Perhaps because the testimony was true enough to hurt. Then again Tomas knew another truth. The mortgage on Milan’s house was held by Montiselle Bank. It was likely that, if Gavinic held his nerve and took responsibility, in a month or two’s time, once the dust had settled and the paparazzi had moved on to another scandal, he would likely find his debt was paid off.

Milan played his part well. When questioned directly, he admitted responsibility without fully confessing intent. Classic football language. “I made catastrophic professional errors, he said. I allowed competitive pressure to distort my judgement. I accept responsibility for operational failures that were mine to oversee and own.” Operational failures, as if he’d misfiled spreadsheets rather than corrupted opponents.

Still, nobody pushed harder. Because everyone inside the room understood an unspoken agreement now existed. Gavinic would absorb the blame. The federation would restore symbolic order. The league would show had been outed. Montiselle would pause, but remain too powerful to stop for long. Football preferred controlled burns. Total fires threatened everyone.

The verdict arrived at 18:11. Dynamo Bresno: stripped of the league title, deducted points, removed from European competition, fined heavily for gross unsporting conduct. Milan Gavinic:

two year touchline ban pending appeal review.

The room remained silent for several seconds afterward. Then the journalists exploded outwards, toward phones and exits like released pressure valves. Outside VFF headquarters, reporters presented updates live into cameras while supporters gathered chanting competing slogans.

Clara moved immediately into crisis-management mode. The machine rolled into operation. Spins and endorsements were placed with press still favourable to the club. Sponsor calls to reassure and commit. Internal and external statements prepared to officially respond to the ruling. Player and staff welfare checks to ensure no one was taking the blame too hard.

Only Tomas remained still. Across the room, Gavinic sat alone while officials packed documents around him. There was no dramatic collapse or tears, just emptiness settling gradually around a man no longer useful. Tomas walked over quietly. “It’s done.” Gavinic nodded once. “You’ll receive settlement terms tomorrow.”

Gavinic laughed softly. “I’m surprised you aren’t invoking gross misconduct or breach of contract.”

Tomas looked at him intently for a moment. “No. Not that way. We look after our own, Milan.”

That almost produced a real smile. Almost. Then Gavinic stood slowly and adjusted his jacket. “What happens to Dynamo now?”

Tomas answered honestly. “We survive. We go again. We rebuild a fallen giant.”

Gavinic looked at him carefully. “And you?”

The question caught him unexpectedly. Because beneath it sat the deeper issue neither man had voiced openly yet: What did survival cost? Tomas glanced toward the corridor where Montiselle representatives already coordinated future strategy. The project would continue. Of course it would. It would do more than survive. It would prosper. Luxury ownership groups did not disappear because of morality. They adapted.

“Honestly?” Tomas said quietly. “I will be the leader the club needs me to be.”

Gavinic absorbed that without satisfaction. Only recognition. “Welcome to Bresno,” he murmured.

The official departure announcement arrived four days later. The language of ‘mutual agreement’ was embraced, with both parties moving forward in different directions. Supporters gathered outside the stadium that night carrying flares through heavy smoke. Some applauded Gavinic’s car as it exited underground parking. Others spat toward it. One supporter held a giant banner reading: MAN OF THE CITY? Police tried to confiscate it but all they achieved was generating photographs that spread nationally.

Inside the executive floor, Tomas, Clara and Milo watched the scenes unfold silently through glass. Milo poured whiskey into crystal glasses. “A disaster,” he muttered.

Clara corrected him immediately. “A contained disaster.”

Milo looked toward Tomas. “And what’s the difference?”

Tomas watched the smoke drifting upward around the stadium exterior. He thought about the old Dynamo history; the workers’ club origins, the political fractures, the decades of identity conflict, near financial ruin and collapse. Maybe this sort of outcome had always been waiting somewhere beneath the surface, ready to bubble up and burst the bubble.

His phone vibrated quietly. A message from a Montiselle executive channel. One line only. VARGA CONFIRMED. ANNOUNCE FRIDAY. Anton Varga. Local boy with industrial east roots. Authentic, disciplined, untainted publicly. Ex-international. A famous tactician who took unfancied Red Star Polvan from obscurity to solid top leaguers. Working abroad until recently but wanting to come home like the prodigal son. A notorious bastard to work with and work for.

The corrective narrative had already begun construction. Milo noticed Tomas reading. “The replacement confirmed?”

Tomas nodded once. Clara exhaled slowly. “Good choice?”

“Yes,” Tomas replied quietly.

“But notice how quickly we moved.” Nobody answered. Because that too revealed something uncomfortable. Even scandal had become operationally efficient inside modern football. Outside, supporters continued singing beneath the floodlit smoke. Some mourning. Some celebrating. Most simply wanting the feeling of belonging to survive the truth.

Tomas stared down toward the darkness below. The title was gone. Europe was gone. Gavinic was gone. And yet Dynamo Bresno remained standing. Perhaps that was resilience. Or perhaps institutions simply became hardest to destroy once enough people confused them with identity itself. Far beyond the stadium, over the western districts of Bresno, fireworks exploded again across the river. Red. Blue. Gold. Beautiful from a distance. Almost impossible to tell whether they marked a funeral or a beginning.
