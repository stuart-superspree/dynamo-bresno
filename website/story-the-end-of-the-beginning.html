The End of the Beginning

A short story from the world of Dynamo Bresno · About 7 minutes

← Short Stories

Three days after Anton Varga’s appointment, somebody spray-painted a single sentence across the old railway bridge entering West Bresno: CHEATS CAN’T CHANGE. By midday the city council had already scrubbed it clean. By afternoon photographs of the graffiti had appeared across various social media streams and message boards. By early evening, Dynamo Bresno’s commercial department was discussing whether the slogan could appear on official or unofficial supporter merchandise, and whether allowing it to spread might soften perceptions among younger audiences.

That was the final stage of a modern scandal: monetisation. The final decision was to go with a variation of YOU CAN’T CHANGE WHO YOU ARE on a new line of original fans merchandise, along with a cheaper counterfeit market version. Both would be made in the same Montiselle factory in Burma.

Anton Varga arrived in Bresno beneath low cloud and renewing optimism. He didn’t want a grand unveiling; in fact he had insisted upon it. No luxury helicopter footage. No cinematic trailer. Just an interview with him sat behind a trestle table covered with a Dynamo tablecloth. That was all he was prepared to accept. It was both his nature and a deliberate effort on the part of the club to show modesty.

After GoulashGate, Montiselle wanted humility for at least six weeks. Preseason was scheduled to be ‘low key media outputs’. Varga stepped from a battered taxi outside the Stade du Cœur de Feu wearing a faded tracksuit and a scowl, carrying his own bag. That mattered to people here. Bresno noticed small things. Especially after the scandal. Supporters gathered beyond the barriers watched him carefully. Some applauded. Some silently filmed. Some looked unconvinced. But nobody laughed and a few applauded. That alone represented progress.

Inside the stadium, Tomas Montiselle waited near the main entrance alongside Clara Jensen and Milo Kolar. Milo spoke first. “He looks grumpy already.”

“He’s inherited a poisoned football club,” Clara replied. “Seems appropriate.” Even Tomas smiled faintly at that.

Varga approached with the measured gait of a man who had spent decades inside difficult dressing rooms. Broad shoulders. Weathered face. Eyes that looked through you, into your soul. He was well known for his desire to analyse people and problems. When he shook Tomas’s hand, the grip felt firm and dry.

“Welcome home,” Tomas said.

Varga looked around the vast stadium bowl quietly. “It’s strange to hear this place called home,” he replied. East Bresno accent still, after all these years. Rail-yard roughness beneath the words. Authentic enough to calm supporters. Genuine enough to unsettle Montiselle. Exactly why Tomas had wanted him. Exactly why certain people above Tomas had not. As they walked toward the executive level, Varga glanced toward the giant slogan mounted beneath the stand roof: PRECISION. PROGRESS. PRESTIGE.

“You keeping that?” he asked.

Milo laughed. “It’s legally required at this point.”

A hint of a smile flickered across Varga’s face. Good, Tomas thought to himself, there is humanity lurking inside the man. That mattered. Inside the boardroom, however, the atmosphere tensed. Football clubs could reinvent their public image quickly. Internally, the media and comms teams had been hard at work determining the next steps. Clara handed Varga the operational briefing pack. Varga flipped through silently. Finally he said, “You’ve got players traumatised, supporters embarrassed, and journalists treating the club like a Netflix crime series.”

“That’s one interpretation,” Clara replied. Varga grunted and carried on reading. Tomas watched him carefully. Unlike Gavinic, Varga did not radiate paranoia or ego. He radiated calm assessment. He looked at problems the way an old mechanic examined a failing engine: rationally and without sentimentality.

“What do you need?” Tomas asked.

Varga answered immediately. “Control.”

Milo snorted quietly. “Good luck.”

Varga ignored him. “No more media interference with training schedules. No fashion documentary crews inside meetings. No commercial obligations before derby weeks.”

“That affects sponsorship deliverables,” Clara said automatically.

Varga looked directly at her. “You hired me because the club became a joke.” Silence. Then, “You can either rebuild the football side properly or just continue producing disasters. I don’t particularly care which. But I won’t do both simultaneously.” Tomas felt something close to relief. Not because Varga was easy. Because he wasn’t. The club needed resistance now.

Not another polished employee.

Milo leaned back in his chair. “You know they’ll turn on you too, eventually.”

Varga shrugged. “That’s football.” No self-pity. No illusion. Just acceptance. That unsettled Tomas slightly because it sounded wiser than everyone else in the room.

---

The city itself remained divided long after the federation ruling. West Bresno defended the club instinctively. East Bresno mocked it relentlessly.

Iron supporters sold fake takeaway menus outside derby pubs featuring items like; ‘Foul Play Pie’, ‘Scandal Soup’, ‘Fixed Ref Fricassee’ and ‘The Chef’s Special Contamination Platter.’ One local restaurant introduced a ‘Gavinic Goulash’ before the pace of events forced them to rename it within forty-eight hours. They changed it to; ‘The Former Manager’s in the Stew.’

Meanwhile national opinion hardened. Dynamo Bresno had become shorthand for modern football corruption throughout Valrekia. Political cartoonists drew Montiselle executives cooking the books. Television comedians wore chef hats and Galvinic goatees during football related sketches.

And yet supporter metrics barely dropped. That intrigued Tomas more than the protests. Because it suggested supporters no longer assessed clubs morally, only tribally. To his mind it followed that, if identity attached deeply enough, almost anything became survivable.

Late one evening, Tomas walked alone through the empty stadium corridors after another sponsorship call with unhappy investors. Montiselle remained publicly supportive. Privately, however, the tone had changed. Questions now arrived attached to metrics, which were in turn attached to timelines. Recovery projections, brand sentiment trajectories and European cup competition re-entry modelling had been the last three he fielded. He was used to the language, but it still occasionally caused him to raise an eyebrow. Why couldn’t they just ask how long wil it take to get over this? Will the brand grow and how quickly? And, will we be playing back in European competitions, because that is where the real money is? No one mentioned morality, or ethics, or doing the right thing.

He stopped outside the trophy gallery. The championship display remained empty. The removed title plate had left behind a slightly cleaner rectangle beneath the lights. The spot where the trophy should have been stood empty. Ghost silverware. For several moments Tomas simply stood there staring at absence.

“You should’ve appealed.” The voice came from behind him. Milo Kolar. White t-shirt stretched by years of good living. Whiskey glass in hand. Signs of exhaustion radiating from him now.

“The federation would’ve destroyed us publicly,” Tomas replied.

“They already did.”

“Yes. But only for a moment and now they stop.”

Milo stood beside him. “You know what’s funny?”

“Probably not.”

“Twenty years ago, this country fixed matches openly and nobody cared.” He gestured vaguely toward the missing trophy space. “Now everybody acts horrified because one manager went beyond the norm to grab victory.”

Tomas almost objected. Instead, he said: “It horrified because people were hospitalised.”

Milo nodded slowly. “Yes. That’s the line apparently.” The words hung unpleasantly between them. Because both men understood football’s moral boundaries shifted constantly according to scale, visibility, and inconvenience. Milo sipped whiskey. “You think Gavinic was completely wrong?”

Tomas looked toward him sharply. “That’s not the same as justified.”

“No,” Milo agreed quietly. “But that’s not what I asked.”

Tomas did not answer immediately. Beyond the gallery windows, Bresno glowed beneath rain and industrial haze. The city had always demanded hardness from people. Survival first.

Reflection afterward. Perhaps Montiselle had underestimated how dangerous that mentality became once merged with elite ambition and modern football money. “I think,” Tomas said slowly, “he became the logical endpoint of an unhealthy environment.”

Milo nodded once. “Exactly.” Neither spoke after that. Because if the environment produced Gavinic once, what prevented it happening again?

…

Two weeks later, Milan Gavinic finally reappeared in public, at a small riverside café in old Bresno where a freelance journalist found him drinking coffee alone. The story and photographs spread nationally within hours. Gavinic looked older. Heavier somehow, both physically and mentally, as if the scandal had aged him years.

The journalist later described him sitting beneath a television replaying Dynamo’s stripped title celebrations on mute while everybody inside the café chose to ignore him. That detail saddened Tomas most. By evening, excerpts from the interview that followed dominated the national media.

“I made catastrophic decisions. I became consumed by pressure. I embarrassed myself, the club and the city. I thought winning justified the means.” Then finally. “But if you believe I acted alone, as a product of a healthy football culture, you understand nothing about modern football.”

That quote detonated across Valrekia. Pundits debated responsibility endlessly afterward. Was Gavinic uniquely guilty or the fall guy for something bigger, more sinister? Or was he merely the man careless enough to expose how elite football already functioned? No consensus emerged. Only noise. Which suited everyone in power perfectly.
